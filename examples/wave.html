The wave equation — 2A: conservative anisotropic membrane

Conservative 2D anisotropic membrane with mixed boundary ports.

2D mixed-boundary anisotropic membrane, energy variables q=grad w, p=rho*w_t, coenergy e_q=T q and e_p=p/rho. This preserves the original model’s four boundary ports and its actual GetFEM GWFL Hamiltonian 0.5*q.T.q, where T is the full tensor parameter.

Physical outward-normal traction: u_N=n.e_q at bottom/right/top; observation y_N=e_p. Left side prescribes u_D=e_p (velocity) and observes y_D=n.e_q (traction). The pH energy balance is

H(t)-H(0) = integral (u_N*y_N + u_D*y_D) dt.

The negative flow-side algebraic rows impose Y_N=e_p and U_D=e_p: this is the same physical sign convention as qualified wave_1d.py. The physical sign of the measured work is audited independently against the original GetFEM Hamiltonian; no success is inferred solely from these formulae.

This tutorial starts from EXACTLY zero, compatible with zero initial Dirichlet velocity and initial normal stress, and uses a left-boundary velocity that is zero at t=0 but nontrivial soon after. The original model, before promotion, passed real MUMPS MPI1/2 10-step TSCN physical and shared-ParaView tests at cf42089d; other LU backends require independent native acceptance.

Scientific context: Haine, Matignon, Serhani (2023), anisotropic heterogeneous wave PFEM, DOI 10.4208/ijnam2023-1005; and Brugnoli, Haine, Matignon (2022) on mixed boundary control.

examples.wave.main()
examples.wave.measure_wave_2d(system)

Measure ORIGINAL GetFEM energy and all four signed port powers.

examples.wave.wave_eq(*, mesh_size=0.2, final_time=0.08, time_step=0.008, factor_solver=None)

Build and integrate the conservative mixed-boundary 2D pH wave.

The time/mesh defaults are intentionally for native installation acceptance, not for a convergence order or published benchmark.

Why this example?

The first tutorial showed a one-dimensional string and its two boundary ports. Our next model is genuinely two-dimensional, heterogeneous and anisotropic, with mixed boundary control: three boundary segments prescribe an outward normal traction, while the fourth prescribes a velocity. We will keep exactly the same materials, mesh and boundary convention when we add an internal dissipative port in example 2B. We will then study the complementary co-energy formulation in example 2C (The wave equation — 2C: co-energy and localized damping).

This page describes the actual code in examples/wave.py. Its snippets are included directly from that file, avoiding manual discrepancies between the tutorial and the program. The validated source is the same for all PETSc solver backends.

The physical model

Let \(\Omega=(0,2)\times(0,1)\) and let \(w(t,x,y)\) denote the transverse displacement of a membrane. We consider

\[\rho(x)\,\partial_{tt} w - \operatorname{div}(T(x,y)\nabla w)=0,\]

where the in-plane tension tensor and areal mass density are

\[\begin{split}T(x,y)=\begin{bmatrix}5+x&xy\\xy&2+y\end{bmatrix}, \qquad \rho(x)=3-x.\end{split}\]

Here \(T\) is not a scalar Young modulus. It is a symmetric, positive-definite anisotropic tensor; both \(T\) and \(\rho\) vary spatially. The lower, right and upper boundaries are \(\Gamma_N\) (GetFEM regions 10, 11 and 12). The left boundary is \(\Gamma_D\) (region 13). Writing \(n\) for the outward unit normal, the controls are

\[n\cdot(T\nabla w)=u_N \quad\hbox{on }\Gamma_N, \qquad \partial_t w=u_D \quad\hbox{on }\Gamma_D.\]

The second condition prescribes velocity, not displacement. This is sometimes called a velocity-Dirichlet port in the port-Hamiltonian literature.

Energy variables, co-energy variables and power

We choose the energy states

\[q=\nabla w,\qquad p=\rho\,\partial_t w,\]

and their conjugate co-energies

\[e_q=Tq,\qquad e_p=p/\rho.\]

Thus \(q\) and \(e_q\) are vectors, whereas \(p\) and \(e_p\) are scalars. The first-order port-Hamiltonian dynamics are

\[\partial_t q=\nabla e_p,\qquad \partial_t p=\operatorname{div}e_q.\]

The physical Hamiltonian is

(1)\[\mathcal H(q,p)=\tfrac12\int_{\Omega} \left(q\cdot Tq+\frac{p^2}{\rho}\right)\,\mathrm dx.\]

In the GetFEM Generic Weak Form Language (GWFL), q.T.q denotes \(q\cdot Tq\): T is the tensor parameter’s identifier, not the transpose operator from Python/NumPy. The actual code therefore correctly uses "0.5*q.T.q".

With our boundary orientation, the collocated outputs are

\[y_N=e_p \quad\hbox{on }\Gamma_N,\qquad y_D=n\cdot e_q \quad\hbox{on }\Gamma_D.\]

The energy exchanged with the environment is

(2)\[\frac{\mathrm d\mathcal H}{\mathrm dt} =\int_{\Gamma_N}u_N y_N\,\mathrm ds +\int_{\Gamma_D}u_D y_D\,\mathrm ds.\]

Our short demonstration uses zero Neumann tractions, with a nontrivial prescribed left velocity. Thus the left-hand boundary supplies the entire mechanical work; the right, top and bottom boundary powers are zero.

From the strong equation to PFEM

Integrating \(\partial_t p=\operatorname{div}e_q\) against a test function \(\varphi_p\) gives

\[\int_\Omega\partial_t p\,\varphi_p =-\int_\Omega e_q\cdot\nabla\varphi_p +\int_{\Gamma_N}u_N\varphi_p +\int_{\Gamma_D}y_D\varphi_p.\]

The first equation is tested without integration by parts:

\[\int_\Omega\partial_t q\cdot\varphi_q =\int_\Omega\nabla e_p\cdot\varphi_q.\]

These two choices yield the partitioned finite-element discretization (PFEM): DG1 for vector strain \(q\), CG2 for scalar momentum \(p\), and DG1 boundary traces. The exact finite-element spaces and material definitions are shown below.

In SCRIMP’s assembly convention, the flow-side algebraic variables for the Neumann velocity observations are ``-Y_B``, ``-Y_R``, ``-Y_T``, and the velocity input on the left appears as ``-U_L``. The corresponding effort-side test terms are negative velocity traces. The signs make the measured powers consistent with (2). They have been checked by native GetFEM/PETSc simulations, not merely assumed from the matrix layout.

Build the real SCRIMP model

To reproduce the snippets in a notebook, begin with these imports and the same tested defaults (the command-line entry point accepts other values):

import numpy as np
import scrimp as S

mesh_size = 0.2
final_time = 0.08
time_step = 0.008
factor_solver = None  # Production default: MUMPS

The four boundary identifiers are taken directly from the Python module, too:

BOUNDARIES = (
    ("bottom", 10, "B"),
    ("right", 11, "R"),
    ("top", 12, "T"),
    ("left", 13, "L"),
)

The following seven sections are taken literally from examples/wave.py:wave_eq(); no duplicate hand-written Python excerpts are maintained in Sphinx. The boundary index tuple BOUNDARIES is defined at module level.

1. Instantiate the DPHS, domain, states and co-states.

system = S.DPHS("real")
system.set_output_name("wave_2d")
system.set_domain(S.Domain("Rectangle", {
    "L": 2., "l": 1., "h": mesh_size,
}, mesh_output_dir=system.get_output_path("mesh",create=True)))

q = S.State("q", "Strain", "vector-field")
p = S.State("p", "Linear momentum", "scalar-field")
system.add_state(q)
system.add_state(p)
system.add_costate(S.CoState("e_q", "Stress", q))
system.add_costate(S.CoState("e_p", "Velocity", p))

2. Define the four mixed boundary ports. Only the left port uses position="flow", since it imposes the velocity instead of the normal traction.

for side, region, suffix in BOUNDARIES:
    # Only the LEFT boundary is velocity-controlled (flow port).
    is_left = side == "left"
    system.add_control_port(S.Control_Port(
        f"Boundary control ({side})",
        f"U_{suffix}",
        "Velocity" if is_left else "Outward normal traction",
        f"Y_{suffix}",
        "Outward normal traction" if is_left else "Velocity",
        "scalar-field",
        region=region,
        position="flow" if is_left else "effort",
    ))

3. Choose the PFEM spaces and material coefficients. GetFEM’s nested GWFL matrix definition is symmetric and represents the actual inhomogeneous tensor \(T\).

for fem in (
    S.FEM("q", 1, "DG"),
    S.FEM("p", 2, "CG"),
    *(S.FEM(f"Boundary control ({side})", 1, "DG")
      for side, _, _ in BOUNDARIES),
):
    system.add_FEM(fem)

# This T is symmetric positive definite throughout [0,2] x [0,1].
# GWFL nested matrices list COLUMNS; it is symmetric here.
system.add_parameter(S.Parameter(
    "T", "Anisotropic membrane tension", "tensor-field",
    "[[5+x,x*y],[x*y,2+y]]", "q",
))
system.add_parameter(S.Parameter(
    "rho", "Areal mass density", "scalar-field",
    "3-x", "p",
))

4. Assemble the Dirac structure and constitutive laws. The rows with dt=True represent the differential part of the port-Hamiltonian DAE. Boundary observations and constraints are algebraic; the signs are visible here.

bricks = [
    S.Brick("M_q", "q.Test_q", [1], dt=True, position="flow"),
    S.Brick("M_p", "p*Test_p", [1], dt=True, position="flow"),
    # Signed algebraic observation in *flow*:
    #   -Y_N = -e_p -> Y_N=e_p (Neumann outputs).
    S.Brick("-M_Y_B", "-Y_B*Test_Y_B", [10], position="flow"),
    S.Brick("-M_Y_R", "-Y_R*Test_Y_R", [11], position="flow"),
    S.Brick("-M_Y_T", "-Y_T*Test_Y_T", [12], position="flow"),
    # Mixed left velocity input: -U_L=-e_p -> U_L=e_p.
    S.Brick("-M_U_L", "-U_L*Test_Y_L", [13], position="flow"),
    S.Brick("D", "Grad(e_p).Test_q", [1], position="effort"),
    S.Brick("-D^T", "-e_q.Grad(Test_p)", [1], position="effort"),
    S.Brick("B_B", "U_B*Test_p", [10], position="effort"),
    S.Brick("B_R", "U_R*Test_p", [11], position="effort"),
    S.Brick("B_T", "U_T*Test_p", [12], position="effort"),
    S.Brick("B_L", "Y_L*Test_p", [13], position="effort"),
    S.Brick("-B_B^T", "-e_p*Test_Y_B", [10], position="effort"),
    S.Brick("-B_R^T", "-e_p*Test_Y_R", [11], position="effort"),
    S.Brick("-B_T^T", "-e_p*Test_Y_T", [12], position="effort"),
    S.Brick("-B_L^T", "-e_p*Test_Y_L", [13], position="effort"),
    S.Brick("-M_e_q", "-e_q.Test_e_q", [1]),
    S.Brick("CR_q", "q.T.Test_e_q", [1]),
    S.Brick("-M_e_p", "-e_p*Test_e_p", [1]),
    S.Brick("CR_p", "p/rho*Test_e_p", [1]),
]
for brick in bricks:
    system.add_brick(brick)

5. Prescribe compatible initial data and controls. We set \(q_0=p_0=0\), so \(e_q=e_p=0\) at the initial instant, and apply

\[u_D(t,y)=0.2\sin(4\pi t)\sin(\pi y).\]

This input also vanishes at \(t=0\) and at the corners of the left boundary. It is already nonzero during the ten-step installation smoke.

# Exactly compatible at t=0 (zero stress and zero velocity).
# The left boundary injects energy immediately after t=0.
for side, _, _ in BOUNDARIES:
    value = (
        "0.2*sin(4*pi*t)*sin(pi*y)"
        if side == "left" else "0."
    )
    system.set_control(f"Boundary control ({side})", value)
system.set_initial_value("q", "[0., 0.]")
system.set_initial_value("p", "0.")

6. Solve the actual DAE. The algebraic initial projection is strict and preserves the prescribed differential data. For the quick installation check we use fixed Crank–Nicolson, \(t_f=0.08\), \(\Delta t=0.008\), and ten accepted time steps, not a five-second publication reproduction.

options = {}
if factor_solver is not None:
    options["pc_factor_mat_solver_type"] = factor_solver
system.set_time_scheme(
    init_step_ts_type="algebraic", init_step=True,
    ts_type="cn", ts_adapt_type="none",
    ts_exact_final_time="matchstep",
    ksp_type="gmres", pc_type="lu",
    t_0=0., t_f=final_time,
    dt=time_step, dt_save=time_step,
    **options,
)
system.solve()

7. Register the real anisotropic Hamiltonian. The two integrals are evaluated by GetFEM on the actual finite-element fields.

system.hamiltonian.set_name(
    "Anisotropic membrane mechanical energy (mixed boundary control)"
)
system.hamiltonian.add_term(S.Term(
    "Potential energy (q dot T q)", "0.5*q.T.q", [1],
))
system.hamiltonian.add_term(S.Term(
    "Kinetic energy", "0.5*p*p/rho", [1],
))

To reproduce the complete program, use the canonical entry point, which encapsulates the steps above (and defines BOUNDARIES):

python -m examples.wave --solver mumps --check --export --output outputs/my_wave_2d

2B — Adding a resistive port: heterogeneous damping

Dissipative 2D anisotropic membrane with mixed boundary ports.

2D mixed-boundary anisotropic membrane, energy variables q=grad w, p=rho*w_t, coenergy e_q=T q and e_p=p/rho. This preserves the original model’s four boundary ports and its actual GetFEM GWFL Hamiltonian 0.5*q.T.q, where T is the full tensor parameter.

Physical outward-normal traction: u_N=n.e_q at bottom/right/top; observation y_N=e_p. Left side prescribes u_D=e_p (velocity) and observes y_D=n.e_q (traction). The physical pH energy balance is

H(t)-H(0) = W_boundary(t) - integral_0^t D(s) ds.

The signed algebraic boundary rows impose Y_N=e_p and U_D=e_p. The additional port obeys f_r=-e_p, e_r=nu*f_r with nu>=0. Thus its positive dissipated power is e_r*f_r=nu*f_r*f_r and dH/dt = signed_boundary_work_rate - integral_Omega(nu*f_r*f_r). The physical sign of the measured work is audited independently against the original GetFEM Hamiltonian; no success is inferred solely from these formulae.

As in qualified 2A, the initial strain and momentum are exactly zero, the left velocity input starts at zero, and the tensor, density, boundary mesh and 10-step CN discretization are unchanged. The only new physics is nu(x)=damping_strength*0.5*(2-x)>=0.

Scientific context: Haine, Matignon, Serhani (2023), anisotropic heterogeneous wave PFEM, DOI 10.4208/ijnam2023-1005; and Brugnoli, Haine, Matignon (2022) on mixed boundary control.

What changes relative to 2A?

This second example introduces one new physical feature: distributed viscous damping of the 2D anisotropic membrane. The independent, executable source is examples/wave_dissipative.py. Its rectangle, material tensor, density, normal directions, four boundary control ports, finite-element spaces for q and p, initial conditions and fixed Crank–Nicolson scheme are identical to those of The wave equation — 2A: conservative anisotropic membrane.

On \(\Omega=(0,2)\times(0,1)\), the equation now reads

\[\rho(x)\,\partial_{tt}w -\operatorname{div}(T(x,y)\nabla w) +\nu(x)\,\partial_t w=0,\]

with the same symmetric positive-definite tension and mass as example 2A and the additional nonnegative, spatially varying damping coefficient

\[\nu(x)=\tfrac12(2-x)\geq0.\]

The damping is nonzero throughout the interior except on the right boundary. We may choose a nonnegative strength multiplier in the executable; the default is damping_strength=1.0. Damping does not change the Hamiltonian:

\[\mathcal H(q,p)=\tfrac12\int_\Omega \left(q\cdot Tq+\frac{p^2}{\rho}\right)\,\mathrm dx.\]

Why add a separate resistive port?

We retain the skew-adjoint interconnection of 2A and expose the dissipation as a new scalar algebraic port (f_r,e_r). The state and co-energy variables are still

\[q=\nabla w,\quad p=\rho\,\partial_t w,\qquad e_q=Tq,\quad e_p=p/\rho.\]

The additional constitutive and coupling equations are

\[f_r=-e_p,\qquad e_r=\nu f_r,\]
\[\begin{split}\begin{pmatrix}\partial_t q\\ \partial_t p\\ f_r\end{pmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 0&\nabla&0\\ \operatorname{div}&0&I\\ 0&-I&0 \end{bmatrix} \begin{pmatrix}e_q\\e_p\\e_r\end{pmatrix}.\end{split}\]

Indeed, e_r=-nu e_p and partial_t p=operatorname{div}e_q-nu e_p. The discrete resistive port introduces two algebraic fields f_r and e_r (both CG2), while preserving the mechanical variables and boundary ports from example 2A.

The dissipation power is positive:

(3)\[D(t)=\int_\Omega f_r\,e_r\,\mathrm dx =\int_\Omega \nu\,f_r^2\,\mathrm dx\geq0.\]

Consequently, for the same boundary work \(W_\partial(t)\) as 2A,

(4)\[\mathcal H(t)-\mathcal H(0) = W_\partial(t)-\int_0^t D(s)\,\mathrm ds.\]

A dissipative system can still gain mechanical energy if its boundary ports inject more power than it dissipates. The correct identity is therefore \(\Delta H=W_\partial-L_\nu\), not the unconditional monotonicity of \(H\).

Build the actual GetFEM/PETSc model

The following code fragments are read directly from examples/wave_dissipative.py; they are not second, manually maintained versions of the solver.

1. Construct the same 2D physical and co-energy states:

system = S.DPHS("real")
system.set_output_name("wave_dissipative")
system.set_domain(S.Domain("Rectangle", {
    "L": 2., "l": 1., "h": mesh_size,
}, mesh_output_dir=system.get_output_path("mesh",create=True)))

q = S.State("q", "Strain", "vector-field")
p = S.State("p", "Linear momentum", "scalar-field")
system.add_state(q)
system.add_state(p)
system.add_costate(S.CoState("e_q", "Stress", q))
system.add_costate(S.CoState("e_p", "Velocity", p))

2. Reuse the exact 2A mixed boundary control:

for side, region, suffix in BOUNDARIES:
    # Only the LEFT boundary is velocity-controlled (flow port).
    is_left = side == "left"
    system.add_control_port(S.Control_Port(
        f"Boundary control ({side})",
        f"U_{suffix}",
        "Velocity" if is_left else "Outward normal traction",
        f"Y_{suffix}",
        "Outward normal traction" if is_left else "Velocity",
        "scalar-field",
        region=region,
        position="flow" if is_left else "effort",
    ))

3. Add precisely one scalar dissipative port:

system.add_port(S.Port(
    "Damping", "f_r", "e_r", "scalar-field",
    dissipative=True, region=1,
))

4. Reuse DG1/CG2 and add CG2 for damping, with the same tensor and density:

for fem in (
    S.FEM("q", 1, "DG"),
    S.FEM("p", 2, "CG"),
    *(S.FEM(f"Boundary control ({side})", 1, "DG")
      for side, _, _ in BOUNDARIES),
    S.FEM("Damping", 2, "CG"),
):
    system.add_FEM(fem)

# This T is symmetric positive definite throughout [0,2] x [0,1].
# GWFL nested matrices list COLUMNS; it is symmetric here.
system.add_parameter(S.Parameter(
    "T", "Anisotropic membrane tension", "tensor-field",
    "[[5+x,x*y],[x*y,2+y]]", "q",
))
system.add_parameter(S.Parameter(
    "rho", "Areal mass density", "scalar-field",
    "3-x", "p",
))
system.add_parameter(S.Parameter(
    "nu", "Nonnegative heterogeneous viscous damping", "scalar-field",
    f"{damping_strength}*0.5*(2.0-x)", "Damping",
))

5. Keep all 20 original 2A weak-form bricks and add exactly five resistive ones.

bricks = [
    S.Brick("M_q", "q.Test_q", [1], dt=True, position="flow"),
    S.Brick("M_p", "p*Test_p", [1], dt=True, position="flow"),
    # Signed algebraic observation in *flow*:
    #   -Y_N = -e_p -> Y_N=e_p (Neumann outputs).
    S.Brick("-M_Y_B", "-Y_B*Test_Y_B", [10], position="flow"),
    S.Brick("-M_Y_R", "-Y_R*Test_Y_R", [11], position="flow"),
    S.Brick("-M_Y_T", "-Y_T*Test_Y_T", [12], position="flow"),
    # Mixed left velocity input: -U_L=-e_p -> U_L=e_p.
    S.Brick("-M_U_L", "-U_L*Test_Y_L", [13], position="flow"),
    # f_r=-e_p, algebraic resistive flow.
    S.Brick("M_r", "f_r*Test_f_r", [1], position="flow"),
    S.Brick("D", "Grad(e_p).Test_q", [1], position="effort"),
    S.Brick("-D^T", "-e_q.Grad(Test_p)", [1], position="effort"),
    S.Brick("B_B", "U_B*Test_p", [10], position="effort"),
    S.Brick("B_R", "U_R*Test_p", [11], position="effort"),
    S.Brick("B_T", "U_T*Test_p", [12], position="effort"),
    S.Brick("B_L", "Y_L*Test_p", [13], position="effort"),
    # e_r=-nu*e_p enters momentum equation.
    S.Brick("I_r", "e_r*Test_p", [1], position="effort"),
    S.Brick("-I_r^T", "-e_p*Test_f_r", [1], position="effort"),
    S.Brick("-B_B^T", "-e_p*Test_Y_B", [10], position="effort"),
    S.Brick("-B_R^T", "-e_p*Test_Y_R", [11], position="effort"),
    S.Brick("-B_T^T", "-e_p*Test_Y_T", [12], position="effort"),
    S.Brick("-B_L^T", "-e_p*Test_Y_L", [13], position="effort"),
    S.Brick("-M_e_q", "-e_q.Test_e_q", [1]),
    S.Brick("CR_q", "q.T.Test_e_q", [1]),
    S.Brick("-M_e_p", "-e_p*Test_e_p", [1]),
    S.Brick("CR_p", "p/rho*Test_e_p", [1]),
    # Resistive constitutive law e_r=nu*f_r.
    S.Brick("-M_e_r", "-e_r*Test_e_r", [1]),
    S.Brick("CR_r", "nu*f_r*Test_e_r", [1]),
]
for brick in bricks:
    system.add_brick(brick)

The extra bricks are M_r, I_r, -I_r^T, -M_e_r and CR_r. Their orientations implement f_r=-e_p and e_r=nu f_r. In particular, adding a negative e_r term by hand to the Hamiltonian would be wrong: the port itself carries the positive dissipated power in (3).

6. Start with the same compatible states and control pulse:

# Exactly compatible at t=0 (zero stress and zero velocity).
# The left boundary injects energy immediately after t=0.
for side, _, _ in BOUNDARIES:
    value = (
        "0.2*sin(4*pi*t)*sin(pi*y)"
        if side == "left" else "0."
    )
    system.set_control(f"Boundary control ({side})", value)
system.set_initial_value("q", "[0., 0.]")
system.set_initial_value("p", "0.")

7. Apply the same strict algebraic DAE initialization and fixed CN step, without pseudo-time relaxation:

options = {}
if factor_solver is not None:
    options["pc_factor_mat_solver_type"] = factor_solver
system.set_time_scheme(
    init_step_ts_type="algebraic", init_step=True,
    ts_type="cn", ts_adapt_type="none",
    ts_exact_final_time="matchstep",
    ksp_type="gmres", pc_type="lu",
    t_0=0., t_f=final_time,
    dt=time_step, dt_save=time_step,
    **options,
)
system.solve()

8. Compute the unchanged physical Hamiltonian:

system.hamiltonian.set_name(
    "Anisotropic membrane mechanical energy (mixed boundary control)"
)
system.hamiltonian.add_term(S.Term(
    "Potential energy (q dot T q)", "0.5*q.T.q", [1],
))
system.hamiltonian.add_term(S.Term(
    "Kinetic energy", "0.5*p*p/rho", [1],
))

Physical qualification: original boundary work minus dissipation

For each accepted CN step, the program measures the original GetFEM Hamiltonian and all four port powers on the mean finite-element state. The resistive power is computed by SCRIMP’s native Port.compute(), and checked independently by a second GetFEM volume quadrature of nu*f_r*f_r. The intended stepwise residual is

\[\mathcal R_k =\mathcal H_k-\mathcal H_{k-1} -\Delta t_k\sum_{\Gamma}P_{\Gamma,k} +\Delta t_k D_k \simeq0.\]

The script also verifies the cumulative (4), the nonnegativity of the actual dissipated power, the resistive flow equation, and the consistency of the 11-snapshot accepted FE history. Because nu f_r has higher polynomial degree than the CG2 space of e_r, the constitutive relationship is discrete weak projection; a pointwise zero L^2 error is not an appropriate acceptance criterion.
